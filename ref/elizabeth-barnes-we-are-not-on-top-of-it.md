# "We Are Not On Top Of It" — Elizabeth Barnes (METR) on the State of AI Risk

> Sometimes people outside the field say things like "The AI situation can't be that bad, there must be experts who are on top of it". As "an expert", I would like to be clear that we are *not* on top of it.

## The thread

Barnes opens by directly countering the common outside view that "the AI situation can't be that bad — there must be experts who are on top of it":

> As "an expert", I would like to be clear that we are *not* on top of it.

She then lays out four key aspects of the situation:

### (1) On track to develop AI systems capable of causing human extinction / permanent disempowerment, possibly within the next few years

> We are likely on track to develop AI systems capable of causing human extinction/permanent disempowerment, quite possibly within the next few years

This is the headline existential-risk claim: the capability trajectory is not speculative — it is the default projection of current progress.

### (2) Things are chaotic and rushed — not on top of the basics, let alone superhuman alignment

> Things are chaotic and rushed; we aren't on top of the basics (models regularly violate user intent, labs train on things they meant to avoid, security probably isn't good enough to prevent adversaries stealing dangerous models) let alone thorny questions of how to control/align superhuman AI

The "basics" are not handled: models violate user intent, labs train on data they meant to avoid, security is probably insufficient to prevent adversaries from stealing dangerous models. And this is before the thorny question of controlling or aligning superhuman AI.

### (3) METR and other independent orgs are woefully under-resourced

> METR (and other independent orgs, as well as safety/security teams at labs) feel woefully under-resourced compared to the scale and pace of AI development - we're struggling to build benchmarks fast enough, keep ahead of latest capability developments, read and respond to all the safety-related claims that AI developers are making, run all the evaluations and assessments that companies + governments are asking us to, plus develop the science needed to assess risks from increasingly capable AIs.

The independent oversight layer — the organizations trying to build benchmarks, run evals, keep up with capability developments, respond to safety claims, and develop the science of risk assessment — is dramatically under-resourced relative to the pace of AI development.

### (4) Any "reasonable" civilization would be going much more slowly and carefully

> IMO, any "reasonable" civilization would clearly be taking things much more slowly and carefully with AI. The benefits of getting upsides of advanced AI a little faster are small compared to the risks of getting it irrecoverably wrong, and we could lower these risks by going slower

This is the collective-action framing: from the perspective of a reasonable civilization, the rate of AI development is not justified by the upside/risk tradeoff. We could lower the risks by going slower, and the benefits of going a little faster are small compared to the risks of getting it irrecoverably wrong.

## The collective action problem

This thread is a direct articulation of a massive collective action problem: the individual incentive to go faster (capture upside, beat competitors, satisfy investors, etc.) dominates the collective interest in going slower and more carefully, even though from a civilization-level perspective going slower would be the rational choice. Barnes frames this as the outside world being surprised at how things are going — but why are they surprised? It's the predictable outcome of uncoordinated competition.

## Limitations of the METR report (thread post 8)

Barnes also used the thread to flag the limitations of the METR report the thread was accompanying:

> This report isn't robust oversight of frontier AI developers by itself. METR has some levers to incentivise companies' participation, including some relevant legislation, but ultimately participants could have pulled out at any time if the result would be contrary to their interests.

> You can view it partly as a pilot exercise of what regulation (or formalized industry standards) could/should require, or what partners/suppliers/customers/employees should demand from frontier developers.

> We clearly need more robust mechanisms than this for providing accountability for AI developers.

She notes the report was intentionally narrow — only "solidly defensible" claims, generally agreed within METR, focused on a narrow set of risks from current systems, and not commenting on "how concerned should we be" or "does something need to be done." The thread was intended to fill that gap with Barnes' personal opinions.

## Scope limitations flagged in the thread (post 9)

Barnes also notes what was explicitly out of scope:

> Firstly, we only consider "AI takeover" / "loss of control" risks: we don't consider risks from human misuse (e.g. AI helping a terrorist make bioweapons), or other harms where the AI is not "deliberately" seeking power (e.g. impacts on mental health or diffuse societal impacts). Within "loss of control" risks, we don't consider "sabotage" threat models (agents subverting AI development and making it easier for future AIs to evade human control). We're just focusing on the "base case" of whether *current* agents could escape human control.

## Why this matters

This thread is a crisp, expert-level articulation of the case for concern about AI risk from someone inside the evaluation/oversight community. It directly contradicts the "experts are on top of it" narrative, lays out the scale of the resource gap, and frames the whole situation as a collective action problem where going slower would be the rational civilization-level choice but the individual incentives point the other way.
